Halfred

Halfred is a console crossword game like Scrabble, but played against a perfect bot.

Demo

Word lists

A few different lists of valid words are provided depending on how restrictive you want the validity criteria to be.

General requirements

All word lists require words to have an English definition on the English Wiktionary, and they exclude all of the following:

  1. Terms spelled with spaces (like per se), hyphens, or other non-letter characters.

  2. Misspellings.

  3. Non-standard words (like anywhen).

  4. Codes (like HWH).

  5. Obsolete words (like beetlehead).

  6. Derogatory, offensive, perjorative, and vulgar words. (As with all the other restrictions, identifying these words depends on them being properly marked by Wiktionary editors, so I can't guarantee that no bad words have slipped through.)

  7. Acronyms (like NASA), initialisms (like BBC) and pseudo-acronyms (like GNOME). Not all initialisms are properly marked as such on Wiktionary, so if a word consists of entirely uppercase letters, optionally followed by "s" or "es" (making it plural), it's not a valid word.

  8. Elongations (like hmmmm).

Main

In addition to the general requirements, the main word list excludes:

  1. Abbreviations (like assn).

Restricted

In addition to the general requirements, the restricted list also excludes:

  1. Abbreviations (like assn).

  2. Archaic words (like disinhume).

  3. Sexual words. (See the disclaimer above on derogatory words.)

  4. Words that, in the real world, occur at a frequency of less than 1 in 100 million according to the Python wordfreq library.

Two-letter words

Two-letter words play a big role in Halfred because they allow you to play alongside an existing word, not just through it. But I found that many of the two-letter "words" in the generated word lists didn't feel like real words. So I've manually curated a list of two-letter words that are incorporated into all lists of valid words. In order to be included, a word had to meet all the general requirements above, it had to have at least one lowercase letter (to filter out the many initialisms), and it had to have at least one sense definition on Wiktionary that met all of the following criteria:

  1. Wiktionary had at least one quotation for the definition. This means that for every valid two-letter word, if you visit its linked Wiktionary page, you can see at least one example of it being used in a real, third-party book, periodical, website, etc.

  2. The definition was not etymologicially an initialism. In other words, it did not come from taking the first letter of each word in a phrase, or the first letter of each part of a longer word. (In the specific case of ok its origin is disputed, and I chose to include it because of its ubiquity.)

  3. The definition carried some meaning. (Some sounds like di are used as meaningless syllables to indicate a rhythm. I considered filler words like um to have meaning, as they let the hearer know the speaker is thinking.)

  4. The definition was not just a verb meaning to say the word itself, or a noun meaning an instance of the word itself.

  5. The definition was not any of the following parts of speech:

    • proper noun
    • prefix
    • suffix

  6. The definition was not a pronunciation spelling or an eye dialect spelling.

  7. If the definition was an abbreviation, then it had a pronunciation different than the full form and also different than just reading its letters individually.

  8. If the definition was only or chiefly used in certain regions or dialects, these regions or dialects represented at least 100 million people.

  9. The definition was not the name of an English letter (or the plural of such a name).

This gives the following 97 two-letter words. For comparison, the official Scrabble dictionary for the United States and Canada has 107 two-letter words.
